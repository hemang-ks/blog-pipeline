# Exemplar notes

## E1 — Building a custom agent harness with Jev (Medium, @bijit211987)
Mode it represents: technical
Observed (Claude): Opens with a concrete failure (an agent "fixes" tests by weakening assertions). TL;DR up top. One loop (propose → validate → judge → authorize → execute → verify) structures both the system and the article. One pseudocode block. About a 5-minute read. Precise about trade-offs.
What I like: It starts with a real engineering failure instead of jumping straight into definitions or product features. It gives the reader a concrete problem, explains why the obvious approach is insufficient, and then introduces the architecture as a response to that problem. I also like the use of strong, conclusion-driven section headings, the emphasis on explaining why each design choice exists, and the use of a simple mental model that readers can remember after they finish the article. Your blogs should follow that same pattern: real problem, architectural insight, tradeoffs, implementation, and lessons learned. That will make them feel like they were written by someone who has actually built and operated these systems rather than by someone summarizing documentation.
Don't copy: What I would not copy is the tendency to make the article feel too linear or tutorial-like, where the reader is simply walked through component after component. Your blogs should have a stronger point of view and more original insight—for example, what surprised you, what did not work, what tradeoff you discovered, or what conventional approach you disagree with based on evidence. I would also avoid excessive bullet lists, generic statements such as “this improves scalability and reliability,” and overly confident claims that are not backed by measurements or primary sources. Most importantly, I would not let your pipeline produce the common AI-blog structure of “What is X → Benefits → Architecture → Best Practices → Conclusion.” Your writing should feel more like an experienced architect explaining a hard problem, the decisions made, the evidence behind them, and what others should learn from the experience.

## E2 — Inside Jev: architecture of a decision model (Medium, @bijit211987)
Mode it represents: technical
Observed (Claude): TL;DR as 5 numbered points. Seven headed sections. Openly states what's unknown ("the exact architecture and training recipe remain undisclosed"). Ends with practical deployment guidance. About a 4-minute read.
What I like: is the way it explains a fairly complex model architecture through a sequence of increasingly deeper ideas without immediately overwhelming the reader. It starts with why the output matters, then moves into shared context, parallel decision branches, probability readouts, calibration, limitations, and finally how those probabilities can drive real actions. That progression is strong because each section answers a natural next question. I also like that the author repeatedly distinguishes what appears likely from what is actually known; for example, the article explicitly says the exact Jev architecture and RLCD training recipe are undisclosed and calls out what the available tests cannot prove. That kind of intellectual honesty is worth encoding into your pipeline. Your blogs should clearly separate confirmed facts, architectural inference, experimental evidence, and your own interpretation. I would also copy the use of concrete examples—like support-ticket routing and cost-based escalation thresholds—because they turn abstract concepts such as calibration and decision probabilities into something an architect can immediately understand and apply.
Don't copy: What I would not copy is how compressed some of the deeper technical explanations become. Sections such as the discussion of causal attention, KV reuse, isolated branches, Brier loss, temperature scaling, and MoE move very quickly and assume the reader already understands several ML concepts. For your blogs, I would slow down at precisely those moments and add a diagram, a small worked example, or a short “what this means in practice” explanation before continuing. I would also avoid building too much of an article around reconstructing an architecture that has not been publicly disclosed; inference can be valuable, but your pipeline should label it prominently and avoid letting plausible architecture gradually turn into asserted fact. Finally, the article is technically strong but somewhat impersonal. Your writing can differentiate itself by adding more of the architect’s perspective: why you would choose one design over another, where you think it would fail at enterprise scale, what you would measure before deploying it, and what surprised you while analyzing it. That would preserve the technical rigor of this article while making your blogs more distinctive and experience-driven

## E3 — Shopify goes native mobile (The Pragmatic Engineer, Gergely Orosz)
Mode it represents: strategy
Observed (Claude): News hook (Shopify reversing on React Native, with AI as the unexpected driver). History first ("why they chose it in 2020"), then "why now". Insider evidence from leadership. Diagrams and comparison tables. Long (about 3,500–4,000 words before the paywall). Paywalled from section 4.
What I like: is the way it combines timeliness, historical context, first-hand evidence, and architectural analysis. It doesn’t simply say “Shopify is moving away from React Native”; it first explains why Shopify chose React Native in 2020, shows that the strategy actually worked for years, and only then explains what changed in 2026. That makes the article much more credible because it avoids the lazy narrative that the previous decision was “wrong.” It also uses direct input from Shopify engineering leaders, concrete metrics such as sub-500ms screen loads and >99.9% crash-free sessions, and specific migration results such as the Shop app being rewritten natively in 12 weeks. Pragmatic Engineer Newsletter I would encode this pattern into your pipeline: establish the original constraint → explain why the old architecture made sense → identify what changed → use primary-source evidence → analyze how the engineering tradeoff has shifted. I also like how the author zooms out from the Shopify story to a broader industry question: whether AI coding agents are changing the economics of abstraction layers themselves. That is exactly the kind of move your blogs should make—start with something concrete like vLLM, llm-d, GPU scheduling, or an agent framework, then extract a larger architectural lesson.
Don't copy: What I would not copy is some of the more headline-driven or absolute language. Phrases such as “Shopify dropped the bombshell” make the piece lively, but they are more newsletter/journalistic than the technical-author voice I think fits you best. I would also be careful with statements like native “naturally” outperforming React Native; even though the article adds qualifications, your pipeline should prefer precise language around workload, implementation quality, platform constraints, and measured evidence rather than broad absolutes. Pragmatic Engineer Newsletter The article is also quite long because it includes several historical side stories—Clubhouse, Airbnb, Notion, Kotlin Multiplatform—which are useful, but your pipeline should apply a stricter test: does this example materially advance the thesis, or is it just interesting context? Finally, I would avoid copying the structure too literally. Your strongest blogs should add more of your own architectural judgment: what changed in the cost model, what new bottleneck replaces the old one, where this approach may break at enterprise scale, and what you would measure before adopting it. In other words, borrow the article’s research depth, chronology, primary-source evidence, metrics, and evolving-tradeoff narrative, but make your own posts tighter, slightly less journalistic, and more explicitly architecture-driven.
